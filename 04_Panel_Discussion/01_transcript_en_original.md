# Panel 2 · Sustainable Finance in Asia – current status and future landscape of transition

[Official conference agenda](https://www.icmagroup.org/events/9th-annual-icma-and-jsda-sustainable-bond-conference/). Kotaro Sueyoshi’s responses at 16:14–22:56 and 48:38–51:41 are translated from Japanese.

Thank you for the introduction. It is good to see a full house and so much interest in the work of the Japan Securities Dealers Association and the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) to promote sustainable finance in Japan and across the region. Our panel has already been introduced, so let us start. We will spend the next hour on sustainable finance in Asia, particularly transition. The preceding keynote mentioned more than 500 projects under way. That is good to hear. A year after ICMA released its Climate Transition Bond Guidelines, with considerable support from Japan, issuance of labelled transition bonds has been modest. But issuance is only one measure. We are seeing developments in policy and in issuers’ frameworks. I would like us to look candidly at what is and is not working and how capital markets can finance the transition in Asia.

Ling, you have worked internationally and have spent many years in Asia. Over the past twelve months, what conversations have you had with issuers and investors about issuing or buying these instruments? How do those conversations compare with what you hear from clients and colleagues elsewhere?

I head Sustainable Capital Markets for Asia-Pacific at BNP Paribas and am based in Singapore. It is good to be back in Tokyo a year after ICMA announced the guidelines. Let me put those twelve months in perspective. Five or six years ago we were on the receiving end in Asia, learning from global investors what a labelled transaction was. By 2022 or 2023, interest was so high that we had to think about greenwashing controls. Some people who wanted to do a labelled transaction did not fully understand what stood behind it. By 2024 Japan was taking its own transition story to international investors as a pioneering sovereign issuer. There was a risk that investors would interpret it differently, and they did. Over time, two-way communication with investors helped.

In 2025 and 2026, markets have become more diverse. Some have stepped back from these topics. In Europe there has been debate about overregulation. In Asia, governments with long-term plans have generally stuck with them through the ups and downs. In the past twelve months, at least three Korean utility companies have followed Japan in taking their transition stories to global investors in US dollars. Investors had questions about whether the activities represented a transition, which standards applied and the potential for carbon lock-in. Overall, investors welcomed the discussion. Some issuers are now preparing to update their frameworks to incorporate transition assets. I hope we will see more momentum from 2027.

There is a loan-market story here, too. Even before the guidelines, Asian shipping and steel companies, for example, used sustainability-linked structures for loan financing. Those conversations tended to be private because of concerns about greenwashing. As investors become more comfortable asking questions, borrowers may consider bringing structures they have used privately to the public market. Across Asia-Pacific, national priorities, stages of economic development and energy systems differ. Countries implement their Paris commitments at different speeds. There is no single formula for the region. What many markets do share is government support for decarbonisation plans. That has helped give companies the confidence to act.

Three things stand out. Government support can mean consistency between an issuer’s pathway and its government’s policy, as well as incentives or legislation. We see that consistency in Japan and some other jurisdictions. Second, there is work on frameworks below the surface that you will miss if you only look at issuance. Third, we need to include other asset classes, especially loans. The Asia Pacific Loan Market Association has worked with ICMA on consistency between transition frameworks for different kinds of credit. That helps lenders and issuers.

Tezuka-san, we hear about Japanese sovereign issuance and more recently about Korea. We hear less often from corporate issuers themselves. JFE Steel was an early mover, well before the ICMA guidelines. What motivated your transition issuance, and what benefits did you see in a sector that is particularly hard to decarbonise?

Thank you for the introduction. I feel some pressure as the only industrial company represented on this panel. Every industry has a different story. Decarbonising steel requires a long period of technology development and substantial capital investment. The options include electric arc furnaces, carbon-recycling blast furnaces and hydrogen-based direct reduced iron. The means of earning a return on these green transformation investments are still being established. We cannot decide to make an investment simply because it is a transition project. As with ordinary capital expenditure, we prioritise projects by how they fit the business, their investment efficiency and their contribution to our future competitiveness.

JFE issued ¥30 billion in transition bonds in June 2022. At that time the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry selected the transaction as a transition finance model project, and we received a supportive second-party opinion. That policy support encouraged us as an issuer. Transition finance serves more than a funding purpose. It gives us a means of explaining to the market how our decarbonisation strategy connects to specific investments. In September 2023, we also raised capital to finance the green transformation strategy shown on this slide [unclear] (12:15–12:28).

I see two benefits. First, we can diversify our funding sources. Some investors find it difficult to offer conventional financing to a high-emitting sector such as steel. They are willing to provide funds where there is a credible, transparent plan and a clearly defined use of proceeds. That connects our need for decarbonisation capital with investors looking for opportunities to allocate their funds. Second, the financing process gives us an opportunity to examine and explain our strategy. Developing the framework, obtaining an external opinion and speaking with investors require us to explain where each investment fits on our path to carbon neutrality and how it will reduce emissions. That process has strengthened our own understanding. One challenge is the lack of a clear financial incentive. Transition finance does not necessarily give us better funding terms. Financial mechanisms that share the risks of investment will be important.

Something struck me about the last column on your slide. For a presentation from an issuer, it said comparatively little about finance and a great deal about implementing your green transformation strategy. You could relate that directly to the company’s financial position and to reducing risk, which echoes a point from the previous panel. Sueyoshi-san, what are the issuers and investors you work with thinking about transition? What barriers do they face? From your experience in Japan and overseas, what lessons could other markets draw?

Good morning. I would like to pick up on the point about looking at the market as a whole. Let me give a couple of examples. In Japan, we started with pilot transactions. Issuers built up a track record in transition finance, and a market developed. The projects on this slide show the range of sectors involved. I will spare you the details, but I want to give you a sense of how much has already been done. When ICMA issued its Climate Transition Bond Guidelines last year, it gave market participants a common language.

What began in Japan is spreading elsewhere in Asia and to the Middle East. The first example on the slide is a financing framework [unclear] (18:37–18:55). Look at what happens on the lending side. An institution can use the framework both to raise funds itself and to finance its customers. That is how money raised in the capital markets reaches the real economy. This framework covers seven sectors. With coverage that broad, you are bound to ask about transition washing. The framework sets out eligible activities and uses a flowchart to check projects against safeguards. The checks cover both the activity and the entity. Look beyond the institution raising the money to the business it will lend to. I think that approach could be useful elsewhere in Asia.

The second example is a Korean power company. The project concerns a switch from coal-fired power to more efficient gas-fired generation. [unclear] (20:44–21:13). The issuer set out safeguards so that investors could see how the project qualified. Some details of the transaction are [unclear] (21:24–21:51), but there were investors prepared to invest if the issuer could demonstrate eligibility. Making that case can overcome a barrier to investment.

What can we learn from these examples? Countries start from different places, and the priorities differ with the circumstances. What they share is the question of how to get from where they are today to net zero. Transition finance has a role here. ICMA’s guidelines give us a way to address local circumstances within a common framework.

Thank you. One point you emphasised was transparency. That is important to establishing credibility, especially internationally. It is encouraging to see a US dollar transaction [unclear] (23:22–23:30). Rahul, transparency is central to your business. Taking a step back, how does Moody’s assess the growth and value of these frameworks? Is it about the substance, the disclosure, or both? What analysis do your clients actually ask you for?

Thank you for finding a youthful picture of me. From a ratings perspective, the transition to a low-carbon future matters for a couple of reasons. What does it mean for credit risk, the cost of capital and future market access? And how are companies responding through their capital expenditure and fundraising, either to mitigate those risks or to unlock opportunities? Consider the transition finance gap. Across sectors from aviation to cement, steel and transport, we see about US$5 trillion of rated debt globally with elevated exposure to climate transition risk. Leaving that risk unaddressed could affect credit quality. Against that, we see about US$10 billion in labelled transition bonds. There are other forms of financing in between, but that puts the gap in context.

I do not think our main problem is a lack of information. There are dozens of taxonomies and far more disclosure and reporting than there used to be. The question is how to interpret that information so it can inform a decision. It is a little like using artificial intelligence in our workflows. The data is the starting point. What you ask and how you interpret the answer matter.

First, are you asking the right question? Historical emissions tell you little on their own about future risk. You also need to understand exposure to policy changes and the viability of the technology. Second, you need regional and sector context. There is no taxonomy box you can tick to establish that an investment represents a credible transition everywhere. We assessed a sustainability framework that included diesel public transport in a part of Southeast Asia with no public transport. That project offered sustainability benefits there. The same project in central Madrid would have been a different proposition. Similarly, the feasibility of moving steelmaking to electric arc furnaces depends on scrap availability. That looks very different in India and the United States.

Third, keep testing your assumptions. When digital infrastructure began attracting attention in bond markets about eighteen months ago, power usage effectiveness was the metric everyone looked at. Now analysts are looking at power, energy sourcing and water together. I expect the questions to evolve further, including who the tenants are, how they use the data, the risk of stranded assets and the social licence to operate. How we interpret information will differentiate both investors financing the transition and organisations like ours assessing it.

At ICMA we sometimes see investors facing more information than they can use. The question is how to make sense of it. Frank, you are based in Asia at a large global sustainable investor. Rahul spoke about local context and the need to keep reassessing as technology changes. How do you approach transition as a sustainable investor? Is it a separate allocation or embedded in everything you do? What do you see in Asia?

This panel brings together issuers, rating agencies and others dealing with labelled instruments. At Amundi, we help investors assess sustainability, and we also have some influence on how they mobilise capital. We look at transition from both perspectives. It is important to our assessment of investments, including labelled instruments. The fragmented approaches Ling mentioned create a challenge for mobilising capital in Asia. When we talk to different investors in Asia, what they understand by transition varies by market. Public money has played a large role. Part of our job is to work out how to bring in commercial investment at scale.

In my experience there is interest in transition financing in Asia. The questions are which asset managers can demonstrate a sustained commitment and what sort of transition plan is being financed. For an asset owner deciding how to deploy money, it also matters where transition fits in its allocations. Only a handful of the Asian commercial investors we speak to have a transition assessment framework they can use in an investment proposal. Others are just starting to understand transition finance. There are investment opportunities. The question is how to structure them so that commercial investors can put money to work.

Nicholas and I were just discussing how to create a structure that allows transition finance to fit into an investor’s asset allocation. When the definition varies from one jurisdiction to the next, it can be hard to know where an investment belongs. For the second round, let us look ahead. Rahul illustrated the size of the gap when he compared US$10 billion in labelled transition bonds with US$5 trillion of exposure. Ling, how can investors start closing that gap and make transition part of their investments? We need to hear from investors as well as issuers. What are you hearing in those discussions?

I spend much of my time in direct conversations with investors, so we hear firsthand how they approach issuers and individual transactions. Over the past few years, investors have shifted their focus from the individual issue towards the issuer as a whole. In a recent survey of more than 100 investors, prominent considerations included the issuer’s transition goals, credible medium-term targets and potential controversies that might put an issuer on an exclusion list. Other considerations included whether targets align with national or global pathways, whether there is enough capital expenditure behind the decarbonisation plan and which indicators the issuer discloses and reports.

We also asked what profiles or labels investors consider when investing. Green bonds ranked first. Other labelled bonds, including social and sustainability bonds, followed. What surprised me was that companies with a credible sustainability profile also featured in the investment universe, ahead of the transition label. Investors are paying attention to what the company actually does.

An Indonesian issuer recently pointed out to us how much the region has achieved in five or six years compared with the time it took Europe. We should recognise that progress and keep going. Last year, as policies changed in major markets, investors became less vocal about engagement and stewardship. Issuers can read that as a sign that transition matters less when putting together their funding plans. In one-to-one discussions with investors, the demand is still there. BNP Paribas’s view is that 2025 was probably the bottom. On the figures we had last week, global labelled bond issuance was up about 10% compared with 2025. We hope growth will continue into 2027. It will take investors speaking up and engaging. A green pricing premium may not be the main incentive, so issuers need to hear what else investors value.

One more practical point concerns second-party opinions. In several transition transactions I have worked on, issuers considering a reviewer have found that the methodology may not give their framework a high score. Those of us using these methodologies every day understand why a transition activity can score below a fully green one. But to an issuer or investor looking at the result on paper, it can appear that the framework is less advanced. There is a gap we could address through education or perhaps through how the methodology is presented.

Rahul, would you like to respond?

If you are financing transition, the market recognises that the activity is not yet fully green. Being transparent and pragmatic about the choices you are making and their opportunity costs can protect you against greenwashing concerns. An external review whose outcome reflects what is actually being financed helps the market understand the investment. I would expect methodologies across the market to keep evolving over the next five or six years.

Engagement and stewardship matter because transition is a process. As we heard on the previous panel, it is like a film rather than a single picture. Tezuka-san, you have issued a transition bond and set out a strategy. To retain credibility with sophisticated investors you need an ongoing dialogue. What goes into maintaining that credibility and implementing the strategy in a way your investors can follow?

We start by explaining the long-term decarbonisation of steelmaking. Carbon neutrality cannot be achieved by installing one piece of equipment. We need to improve energy efficiency and operations in existing processes while developing and introducing technologies such as electric arc furnaces and hydrogen-based steelmaking. These steps take time. The chart on the screen is the Japanese government roadmap that Ito-san showed in his presentation. It illustrates a journey of several decades.

With investors, we explain how many tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions a particular facility can reduce, but we also explain where that investment fits in the long-term pathway, what investment it leads to next and on what timescale. Investors need to understand the conditions under which we can invest. Transforming a blast-furnace steelmaking business depends on an affordable and stable supply of electricity and hydrogen, public support, carbon pricing and the development of a market for green steel. The question goes beyond whether a technology can technically reduce emissions. Investors need to understand what it would take to deploy it at scale and build a sustainable business around it.

Until a green or transition financing is redeemed or repaid, we report annually on the allocation of proceeds and, to the extent possible, the environmental benefits. For loans, we report directly to lenders. We also discuss progress on our transition strategy and changes in business conditions in our integrated and sustainability reports and at our annual environmental management strategy briefing, which our chief executive conducts.

Credibility does not require us to stick to the original timetable regardless of what happens. Steel’s transition depends on technology development, energy supply and the emergence of a market for green steel. We can remain committed to the long-term goal while updating the plan when assumptions change. We need to keep explaining what changed, why it changed and how the revised plan remains aligned with that goal.

Given how much your strategy depends on technology and innovation, updating it over time makes sense. It is good to hear that the chief executive is personally involved. Sueyoshi-san, let me turn to the cross-border challenge. Different countries understand transition differently, and have different starting points, priorities and levels of ambition. What has your experience been, and how would you address those differences in building a genuinely cross-border transition bond market?

Thank you. As you say, countries differ in their energy mix, industries and economic circumstances. Mr Tezuka has just illustrated how much there is to understand about even one sector. It is a lot to ask an investor to understand every sector in every market. His chart looked ahead to 2050. How can a plan earn investors’ trust over that length of time? They need some predictability, and I think three things can help.

First, we need a common language. A great deal of work has gone into developing one. Second, policy needs to complement the guidelines. Within each country, look at a particular industry or region and ask what technology it needs and what policy would support it. Use those policies alongside the Climate Transition Bond Guidelines.

Third, the way bonds are marketed differs from one country to another. I took up this role in April, and that has struck me in my conversations since then. If you have only a day to market an issue, can you really explain a transition in that time? Start talking to investors well before the transaction. It takes repeated conversations to build understanding across the market.

Take the Korean example again. The switch is from coal to gas. Investors will have different views about gas. We need to explain the policies that will take the country from that point to net zero and give investors the information to judge what that means for them. Those three things can give everyone a clearer view of what lies ahead. I hope ICMA will help bring the parties together.

Thank you for those practical ideas. One I will follow up on is how to integrate the Climate Transition Bond Guidelines into regional work [unclear] (51:55–51:57). Rahul, you face something similar. You need a methodology that works globally for your business and your clients, yet you have to recognise the different national climate commitments and taxonomies. How do you strike that balance?

Local relevance is critical. But we need to anchor our analysis in some common principles, and the safeguards in ICMA’s Climate Transition Bond Guidelines help us do that. From what we have seen over the last two or three years, I would highlight three features of successful transition plans. First is commercial viability. Can the company translate its proposed investment into dollars saved through efficiency or dollars earned from new revenue streams? If there is a return on investment, the plan is more likely to survive a change in the political or credit cycle.

Second are external dependencies. To me the most important differentiator is whether the company can actually implement the plan on the ground. Can it secure the investment and get the critical materials it needs? Gas turbines are one example of a bottleneck. Target ambition alone will not answer those questions. Third is management quality, which will be familiar to credit investors. Markets and politics change, and incentives can be volatile. Can management navigate those cycles? Look at how transparent the plan is, whether there are interim targets and clear retirement dates for existing infrastructure, and the quality of accounting and reporting. Those are signals that management can see a transition through.

That brings us back to fundamentals of accounting and governance. Frank, the investor ultimately decides where the money goes. What can asset managers and asset owners do to mobilise more transition capital? And how can ICMA, underwriters, external reviewers and the rest of the market help investors make and justify those decisions?

I can speak for Amundi. We have to define our transition assessment framework clearly. A company’s transition is nonlinear and depends on context. Investors and issuers both need to understand the distinctions we make. Our framework places companies in different categories, including inhibitors, laggards, companies in transition or improving, and transition enablers. Defining those categories gives us a basis for engagement and lets investors and issuers understand where we are coming from.

Collaboration across the market is essential to mobilising capital. Blended finance is an encouraging example. It takes issuers, originators, rating agencies, governments and regulators working together to make a structure work. In April we launched the Global Green Bond Initiative. It is a blended finance structure of around €3 billion with several features to mobilise capital. The portfolio invests in emerging-market green bonds, so ICMA’s framework is part of what makes it work.

The underlying portfolio can include holdings below investment grade. We are looking at an average rating around triple-B, but the blended finance structure and the catalytic capital provided by development institutions enabled Moody’s to rate the fund A2. That makes a difference to asset owners. Many insurers cannot buy unrated or below-investment-grade securities directly. Pooling emerging-market labelled bonds within a structure that carries its own investment-grade rating can make them accessible to commercial investors. That is one example of how the different participants can work together.

Amundi’s track record in emerging markets was also an important credit consideration when we looked at the rating of the fund.

That is a good place to end and gives us more work for next year. Blended finance is an increasingly active part of the market and will be important for transition finance. Thank you to the panellists and to everyone for your attention. I look forward to continuing the conversation.
