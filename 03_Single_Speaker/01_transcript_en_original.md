# Guest speech · Sadanori Ito, Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry

[Official conference agenda](https://www.icmagroup.org/events/9th-annual-icma-and-jsda-sustainable-bond-conference/).

Thank you for the introduction. My name is Sadanori Ito. I am one of the officials responsible for green transformation, or GX, at the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. I would like to bring you up to date on GX policy.

As you know, countries were unfortunately unable to reach agreement at COP31. What struck me at the conference was that each country faces a version of the same question. How do you pursue decarbonization and economic growth while keeping your energy supply secure and resilient?

In Japan, the Kishida administration introduced the GX policy framework in response to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the resulting surge in gas prices. We designed it to achieve three objectives together. We want to cut emissions, secure our energy supply and grow the economy.

A series of cabinet decisions and the GX Promotion Act provide the basis for this policy. We attach particular importance to consistency and predictability. The Takaichi administration has maintained that course.

The energy crisis in the Middle East exposed a vulnerability in Japan’s energy supply. We import more than 90% of our crude oil from the Middle East and depend heavily on the route through the Strait of Hormuz.

Another change the world has seen over the past year is the rise in demand for electricity from AI and the data centres that support it. Japan draws up a Strategic Energy Plan every few years. The sixth plan projected a substantial fall in electricity demand as our population declined.

The seventh plan, adopted last year, changed that outlook. It projects that AI will drive electricity demand up. We are entering an era when the electricity that powers AI will help determine a country’s competitiveness.

As I said, countries everywhere are trying to solve a difficult equation. Europe, for example, has maintained its commitment to carbon neutrality. At the same time, it has placed increasing emphasis on reconciling that objective with industrial competitiveness and economic growth.

South Korea has announced a national strategy of its own, K-GX. My sense is that the world is converging on the idea we have advocated in Japan’s GX policy, that energy security, economic growth and decarbonization must be pursued together.

This summer the Japanese government decided to strengthen GX further. Two things are necessary. First, we still have to use fossil fuels, so we need to make their supply more reliable. Second, we need to accelerate and strengthen GX itself. Both will make us more resilient.

In August the Minister of Economy, Trade and Industry presented the outline of this strengthened policy. We call it Power GX.

On the supply side, the Diet is now considering legislation to reduce our exposure to disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. It would create an incentive to procure supplies by routes that avoid the strait. Naphtha supply also became a concern. Measures are under way to make procurement more reliable, including strengthening reserves.

On the right of the slide is the GX side. We are looking at specific measures to expand renewable and nuclear energy. By the end of this year we intend to set out the government’s strategy in a Power GX Declaration, with more detail on those measures.

Companies play the central role in GX through their investment decisions. When they invest in GX, particularly here in Japan, that spending can both address climate change and contribute to economic growth. We support investment in priority areas. The government’s growth strategy for Japan highlights eight areas related to GX.

Examples include perovskite solar cells, next-generation geothermal power, offshore wind, hydrogen and next-generation nuclear power, including small modular reactors. We support the development of these energy technologies. We are also promoting the transformation of industry, including green chemicals. Across these areas, we are putting together support that runs all the way from technology development through capital investment to creating demand for the resulting products.

There is also investment across entire regions. We created a GX strategic regions programme so local authorities across Japan can make use of assets they already have. These include local sources of carbon-free power and the industrial complexes that drove Japan’s rapid economic growth.

One category of the programme supports data centres together with the carbon-free power that will supply them. It covers nine areas across Japan, from Hokkaido to Kyushu.

The aim is to decarbonize large clusters of data centres. Over the medium to long term, putting an appropriate price on carbon will be important for GX. Japan has now launched its emissions trading system, the GX-ETS.

This year is the stage at which companies carry out their own assessments. Full-scale allocation of allowances and trading are due to begin next autumn. We would particularly welcome the cooperation of those of you in Japan’s securities industry as this gets under way.

I said that corporate investment is at the heart of GX. Two other elements are needed for it to get under way in earnest. One is the policy framework the government puts in place. The other is finance. Unless the financing is there, those investments will not go ahead. Government and the financial industry in Japan have made the case together for transition finance. I feel that the idea is beginning to gain acceptance around the world.

We should also remember Japan’s issuance of GX Economy Transition Bonds to support this work.

These were the world’s first sovereign transition bonds. Issuance has gone smoothly so far with considerable help from the securities industry. I am also deeply grateful to ICMA for its work on the Climate Transition Bond Guidelines and for its support in many other ways. It is no exaggeration to say that the GX Economy Transition Bonds are the foundation of Japan’s GX policy.

We want to continue issuing these bonds with your help and to use the proceeds to support investment up front.

Finally, GX and transition finance extend beyond Japan. We attach particular importance to what happens in Asia. The Asia Zero Emission Community, or AZEC, also began under the Kishida administration. AZEC has now held its fourth leaders’ meeting, with presidents and prime ministers from across Asia taking part themselves.

The leaders discuss how to advance GX and transition finance in the region. Prime Minister Takaichi co-chaired the meeting with President Marcos of the Philippines. Japanese companies are already using the AZEC framework to get energy transition projects under way in Asia.

There are now more than 500 projects. To return to my earlier point, finance has a critical part to play if GX is to develop in Japan, across Asia and around the world. I hope the Japanese government can work with the financial and business communities here and with the financial community abroad to advance GX and contribute to economic growth, energy security and decarbonization worldwide. I hope today will help bring that about. Thank you for listening.
