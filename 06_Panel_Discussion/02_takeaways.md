- Tokyo’s Sadaaki Shimizu gave three reasons for issuing a dedicated resilience bond overseas: secure stable funding for a multiyear infrastructure program, explain Tokyo’s disaster preparations to international investors and encourage other issuers to develop the market. After two overseas issues, Tokyo planned its first domestic resilience bond, with pricing expected later in November.
- Investor expectations for adaptation reporting already differ. Shimizu said some investors wanted Tokyo to quantify damage avoided, while others understood the calculation was difficult and would accept figures on completed facilities.
- A Tokyo Metropolitan Government film showed underground reservoirs temporarily holding river water when heavy rain threatens to overwhelm waterways. It reported fewer flooded homes during typhoons of comparable size over the past 15 years, an observed outcome alongside the city’s projections for future rainfall.
- Crédit Agricole’s Daisaku Chabata expected public issuers to lead dedicated resilience bond issuance because many projects involve infrastructure. He also saw a route for companies through existing green bond frameworks, giving the example of a railway operator investing in rails that can withstand extreme heat.
- Yuji Orui of Rating and Investment Information said a project’s purpose determines how it fits the ICMA Principles. Climate adaptation may fit a green framework, while seismic retrofitting or protecting vulnerable communities may call for social criteria or a combination.
- Japan’s Masato Yasuda showed how one project can serve mitigation and adaptation objectives: solar panels and storage batteries can keep evacuation shelters operating during a power cut. He also cited heat-tolerant rice varieties and heatstroke prevention as responses to risks already affecting Japan.
- The Philippine SEC’s Grace S. Ayson said most Philippine adaptation funding still comes from national and local government. She identified a shortage of bankable projects: some local authorities need technical help to develop proposals, and private investors struggle to measure returns that take years to materialize.
- Ayson described voluntary ASEAN guidance that connects agriculture, water and waste management, and construction and real estate to four hazards: flooding, drought, sea-level rise and extreme heat. Regional consultations let regulators share methods while allowing for different local risks and institutional capacity.
- Chabata said impact estimates depend on the likelihood, extent and location of the hazard; a tonne of avoided CO₂ has no direct equivalent in resilience finance. Orui said an external reviewer must explain why a project fits its locality as well as how it meets published criteria.
- Bloomberg could link labelled bond allocations to the local hazard, the infrastructure financed, observed outcomes and assumptions used to estimate losses avoided. Investors could then distinguish an issuer’s count of completed reservoirs from an estimate of the number of homes protected under a specified storm scenario.
