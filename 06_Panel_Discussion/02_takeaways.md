- Philippine SEC representative Grace Ayson said national and local governments still finance most adaptation in the Philippines. The People’s Survival Fund accepts proposals from both local governments and community organizations, with the Climate Change Commission providing technical support and evaluating proposals.
- Ayson identified two barriers between adaptation needs and investable projects: some local governments lack the technical capacity to prepare proposals, while private investors face long time horizons and returns that are difficult to measure. Developing a stronger pipeline of bankable projects was her priority for the coming year.
- Ayson said the Philippine Sustainable Finance Taxonomy gives issuers, lenders and investors a common basis for classifying activities that contribute to adaptation and mitigation. The central bank, securities regulator and Insurance Commission helped develop it.
- Ayson cited separate roles for taxonomy and bond guidance in the Philippines. The taxonomy classifies activities, while bond guidelines address questions such as how issuers manage proceeds and report on their use.
- Ayson described voluntary ASEAN guidance that connects agriculture, water and waste management, and construction and real estate to four hazards: flood, drought, sea-level rise and extreme heat. It covers both nature-based and technology-based responses.
- Ayson included resilient affordable housing, sanitation and reforestation among the potential uses of resilience finance. An assessment limited to flood-control infrastructure would miss investments that protect essential services and natural systems.
- Ayson said the ASEAN Capital Markets Forum uses consultations and a rotating chair to sustain work across national regulatory regimes. Members can carry an initiative from one chairmanship into the next while adapting regional guidance to local conditions.
- A Tokyo Metropolitan Government film reported that fewer homes flooded in typhoons of comparable size over the past 15 years following flood-control measures. The city uses underground reservoirs to hold rising river water temporarily where surface space is scarce.
- Tokyo’s film distinguished two physical risks with different engineering responses: the city is reinforcing underground storage in anticipation of heavier rainfall and raising coastal defenses in anticipation of sea-level rise.
- Bloomberg could link a labelled bond’s project allocations to the relevant hazard, intervention and reported outcome, such as homes flooded during comparable storms. Investors could use those fields to find issuers reporting an observed change and assess how comparable the underlying events were.
